Overview

The Project

Devgon Industries runs its books through accounting software that tracks invoices and totals well — but has no idea what's actually happening to inventory.

I designed Stockflow as a private, mobile-and-tablet-friendly web app that sits alongside the client's existing accounting workflow — connecting sales, inventory, customers, and smart alerts into one place, with sync back to Zoho so nothing lives in two disconnected systems.

I worked as the sole UX/UI designer on this project, and used AI-assisted "vibe coding" to build and iterate on the backend myself — meaning I owned the product from research through a working, shipped system.

The Problem

Sales Were Tracked.
Stock Was a Mystery.

The client's accounting software recorded every invoice and every total sale — but told him almost nothing about the products behind those numbers.

No Visibility
Stock levels unknown
No way to see how much of any product was actually on hand at a given time
No Signal
Reorder timing was guesswork
Fast vs. slow movers weren't distinguished, so reordering happened too late or too early
No Pattern
Customer habits were invisible
No record of what each customer regularly bought or when they'd likely order again

How might we give a small business owner one accurate, always-current view of stock, sales, and customer behaviour — without asking him to abandon the accounting software he already relies on?

2
Discovery & Research

Watching How the
Business Actually Runs

I talked directly with the client about where the pain was, then observed his existing Zoho accounts to understand what data already existed and what was missing.

Key Research Insights
Inventory lived nowhere accurate
Zoho recorded that a sale happened, but stock-on-hand per product wasn't tracked anywhere reliable — it was reconstructed from memory or spot-checks.
No sense of customer rhythm
The client could see who bought what once, but had no structured way to know that a given customer reorders roughly every 80–90 days — until they were already calling.
Staff accuracy was an open question
Without a consistent record of what was sold and at what quantity, the client had no easy way to confirm sales were being logged correctly at the point of entry.

4
Ideation & Design Decisions

Four Decisions
That Shaped the System

Every decision traced back to one goal: make the thing that's costing the client money today impossible to miss.

Decision 1 — Alerts ranked by financial impact, not just count
Rather than a flat notifications list, alerts are split into "Chase these first" (revenue-generating, like a customer due to reorder) versus "Needs you today" (loss-prevention, like out-of-stock items). The framing itself teaches prioritization.
Decision 2 — Customer segmentation by behaviour, not alphabet
Customers are filterable by status — owes money, reorder due, not ordering, no orders yet — so the owner can act on a segment instead of scanning a full list.
Decision 3 — Reorder cadence per customer, not a generic rule
Instead of a single global "reorder after X days" setting, the system learns each customer's own buying rhythm from their order history and predicts their next likely order individually.
Decision 4 — Sit beside accounting, not replace it
Stockflow syncs sales and invoice data with Zoho rather than asking the client to migrate his books — reducing adoption risk and keeping one system as the source of financial truth.

6
Build Process

Designing and
Building It Myself

With no dedicated engineer on this project, I used AI-assisted "vibe coding" to translate my own Figma decisions directly into a working product.

Design-to-build in one loop
Because I owned both the interface decisions and the implementation, I could test a flow, feel where it broke, and fix the structure the same day — no handoff lag.
Grounding the build in real Zoho data
Rather than designing against sample data, the catalogue and customer records were imported from the client's actual Zoho account from day one, so edge cases showed up early.
Mobile-and-tablet-first by requirement
Since the client explicitly needed phone and iPad support, every screen was designed and tested at that scale first, with desktop treated as secondary.

Reflection

What This Project
Taught Me

Designing for one real business, with real stakes, is a different discipline than designing for a hypothetical user.

Designing around an existing system, not against it
The right answer wasn't to replace the client's accounting software — it was to design something that made that software more useful without asking him to change how he already works.
Alerts are a design problem, not a feature checkbox
The difference between a useless notification feed and a genuinely useful one came down to ranking by financial consequence — a decision made in the interface, not the database.
Owning the build changes how you design
Vibe-coding the backend myself meant every design decision had to be one I could actually implement and defend — it kept the system honest and grounded in what was buildable.
Real data surfaces real problems early
Designing against the client's actual Zoho export from the start surfaced messy, real-world edge cases that a clean sample dataset would have hidden until launch.

The best compliment this project could get isn't a compliment at all — it's the client no longer needing to remember anything, because the system remembers it for him.

— Inayat
