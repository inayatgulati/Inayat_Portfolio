The short version
  1. The brief. Coast Capital arrives after the financial decision is made. We built an app that arrives before it: one that derives what a thing truly costs instead of asking a 23-year-old to already know.
  2. My part. The UI across 32 screens and the eight-step flow they hang on. Research and client strategy were my teammates'.
  3. Then I built it. Rebuilding the file as working code surfaced ten defects in my own work, six of them a number disagreeing with another number, plus a palette the client has never used, and a brand blue that fails WCAG AA at 2.46:1 on our own dark surface.
  4. Then I finished it. Fourteen screens for the parts we drew and never built: the month her hours get cut, the goal that stalls, the transfer that fails, the tab that led nowhere.

Sections 0–5 are the team project as presented. Sections 6–8 are solo work done afterwards: speculative product design, with illustrative figures rather than live data.

Overview

The Problem

Coast Capital Savings is one of Canada's largest credit unions. Its own stated pain: it gets involved only after the member has already made a financial decision.

Our audience was young Canadians, 18–32, saving for a first car, an emergency fund, or their own place. We surveyed 25 of them, and the finding reframed the project: tracking wasn't the problem, confidence was. Budgeting apps are good at showing what already happened. None of them answer the question people actually care about: can I actually afford this?

Only 22% felt prepared for an unexpected expense, the lowest score in the survey, and the one the app would end up answering least.

So the reframe wasn't show people more data. It was a shift in mental model: from "my banking app shows me what already happened" to "my banking app helps me understand what I can afford before I commit." Starts from my context · tests risk early · gives me a next step.

What she changed about the design
Maya was defined by my teammates' research. My job was to design against her. Two decisions came straight from her situation: the app derives the number rather than asking for it, because she doesn't have it. The true cost arrives at the midpoint of the sequence, because a person with $950 spare needs the bad news early enough to act on it, but not so early that the app has said nothing useful yet.
The business case

A credit union does not fund an app because it is well designed. Coast Capital's stated pain, that it gets involved only after the member has already made a financial decision, is a commercial problem before it is a design one: the car loan, the insurance referral and the decades of membership that follow are all decided in the weeks before anyone walks into a branch. An app that is useful during that window is how the credit union gets into it.

The value proposition. For Maya: know what the thing actually costs before committing to it. For Coast Capital: be present at the decision rather than after it, and be the institution that told her the true number when no one else did.

The member loan rate. On the $14,750 she'd borrow, about $20 a month, roughly $1,200 over five years. The rate is the product; the app is what makes it arrive in time to matter.

What membership has already given back: a lower rate, no monthly fee, a share of the profit. v1 never showed it. v2 leads with it, because an unstated benefit is an unfelt one.
$0
Advisor commission
Advisors are salaried, so "don't buy the car yet" is something they are free to say. The business model is what makes the app's advice credible, so the design states it out loud.

This is also the constraint that killed my first round of improvements. That version was an acquisition story: join, share your plan, what membership is worth. Commercially legible, and wrong: it monetised the moment instead of earning it. The value is created when she can afford the thing. The return follows from that, not the other way round.

This case study splits in two. The first half is the work as it was presented. The second half is what I changed afterwards, once I could see it clearly — and that half is the part I'd want to be judged on.

2
User Flow

The Sequence
Is the Product

The app's whole credibility rests on one sequence — the eight-step plan builder.

The obvious version of this app asks for a target number and draws a progress bar. I structured it the other way round: the app asks a series of small, answerable questions and derives the number. The user never has to know the answer in advance, which is the entire reason they'd need the app at all.

The sequence runs what you want → what it actually costs → what that means monthly → what that leaves you. I put the true-cost reveal at the midpoint. Late felt like a punchline at the user's expense; first felt like a lecture before we'd earned the right to give one.

How the sequence got here

The flow went through four rounds before it reached these eight steps: 8 → 10 → 10 → 7, expanding while I worked out what the app was, then contracting once I knew. Three of those cuts decided what the product is. The rest was tidying.

The eight steps
01
What are you saving for?
Goal type, asked before any number is requested.
02
See where you stand
The app reads money in, money out, and what's left, instead of asking her to know it.
03
A suggested plan
A first draft to react to. Reacting is easier than authoring.
04
Change anything you like
Sliders for target, timeline and contribution, recalculated live.
05
Costs you should consider
The true-cost reveal: insurance, fuel, maintenance, parking, registration.
06
A personalised estimate
The same costs recomputed against her age, mileage and area.
07
A contribution path
Three monthly amounts, each showing what she keeps.
08
Save the goal
The plan becomes a tracked goal on the Goals tab.
3
Interface

Five Tabs,
One Question Each

Home answers am I okay? · Budget answers where did it go? · Goals answers am I getting there? · Risks answers what should I worry about?

I held each tab to a single question, because the alternative, one dashboard answering all four at once, is exactly how banking apps become unreadable.

The visual system had to do two contradictory things: stay calm enough that money anxiety doesn't spike, and stay serious enough to be trusted with money. Dark surface, one warm accent, and a consistent card language so every screen reads as the same object repeated. Numbers large and tabular; supporting copy small and quiet. Nothing shouts except the one figure that matters.

The piece I was most sure of
📡
Risk Radar
Instead of only reporting the past, it names things that are becoming problems: a goal that has stalled, a subscription that quietly rose, a safety net too thin. It turns a ledger into something that watches your back.
After the final presentation

I rebuilt all 32 screens as working code — and it exposed my own work

A static Figma frame lets you draw a progress bar at whatever width looks right. A coded one makes you state the number. That is the point where this stopped being a presentation and started being a product.

5
Rebrand

I'd Designed It in Colours
the Client Doesn't Use

InScope was green and gold. Coast Capital's actual brand is Blue Ribbon, Downriver and Alabaster.

Blue Ribbon #0056FF Downriver #0F2A63 Alabaster #FAFAFA

Fixing it properly meant restructuring, not recolouring. I pulled every colour into a token layer, so the palette lives in one block and all 32 screens reference it. Rebranding became one edit instead of thirty-two, which is what the switcher in the corner of this page is actually demonstrating.

The finding that only appears when you build it

Coast Capital's brand blue fails WCAG AA against the dark card surface. Their primary colour cannot carry the accent role on a dark background. So I built two valid answers instead of guessing.

2.46:1
Fails AA
The authentic #0056FF on the dark navy card. Text needs 4.5:1, and the client's own primary can't carry the accent role here.
5.55:1
Dark theme
#6FA5FF, Blue Ribbon lifted just far enough to pass, while still reading as Coast Capital blue.
5.56:1
Light theme
The authentic #0056FF, untouched. On a light surface the real brand colour passes on its own.
What measuring everything else turned up

Once the palette had names I could test all of it, not just the accent: every semantic colour against every surface it actually lands on. That caught two more failures in the original green theme I had never measured: its "on track" green at 4.19:1 and its alert red at 3.00:1, both under the 4.5 minimum, both shipped in the presented file. I have left them in place. Switch to Original green and they are still there. The point of this section is not that the new palette is clean; it is that a colour you have not measured is a colour you do not know, and I had thirty-two screens of them.

Both Coast Capital themes now pass on every surface: accent, muted, warning, success, danger and body text, measured against card, raised card and page background alike. Two of those tokens needed correcting after the first pass, because a colour can pass on a white card and fail on the tinted one two rows down.

6
New Work

We Drew Four Stages
and Built Three

The sharpest finding didn't come from the screens. It came from holding them against our own value-path diagram.

We designed to a four-stage path: See where I stand → Decide → Commit → Live with it, with three loops drawn back into it: "$780 is too much", "my hours got cut this month", and "goal reached, what's next?"

Roughly twenty of the thirty-two screens are the eight-step planner. That path ends the moment she commits. "Live with it" is served by one screen, and none of the three loops we drew were ever built.

Our two lowest survey scores, 22% prepared for unexpected expenses and 29% confident around fluctuating hours, are both "Live with it" problems. And the app's only answer to either is step 7 of onboarding: a hypothetical, seen once, before she commits.

The app rehearses the bad month. It has nothing for the morning the bad month actually arrives, even though Maya's own stated pain is irregular hours, and our own diagram has an arrow labelled "my hours got cut this month" pointing straight back at a screen that doesn't exist.

Closing the loops · each caption names the score or loop it targets
7
Unglamorous States

When the App Speaks,
and When It Breaks

Thirty-two screens contained no notification design and no failure states. For this product specifically, both are load-bearing.

Risk Radar is a tab you have to remember to open. The product's promise is catching things early: a stalled goal, a subscription that rose, a runway falling below target. None of that helps if she only sees it when she happens to open the app. Coast Capital's own stated pain is that it "involves only after the member has already made a financial decision." A notification is the mechanism by which the app is present during the decision. Without one, the core claim has no delivery vehicle.

And it is an auto-transfer product. $360 leaves chequing on a set day each month. For a user whose stated pain is fluctuating hours, a month where that transfer can't clear isn't an edge case — it's the expected case, eventually.

The rule these four screens are written to
✂️
It tells her something she doesn't already know
No daily spend summaries, no streaks, no encouragement with no information in it.
➡️
It arrives with a next step, or it doesn't arrive
A notice she can't act on is just anxiety delivered on a schedule.
🤝
A short month is never a personal failing
Her hours got cut. That is a fact about her employer, not about her character.
🤫
In a quiet month it sends nothing at all
The app earns the right to interrupt by being useful, and stays quiet otherwise.

9
Reflection

What I'd Do Differently

Building the thing I had drawn taught me more than drawing it did.

Check the screens against the diagram
We drew a four-stage value path with three loops and then built a linear funnel. Nobody noticed, because every individual screen was defensible. Holding the screen list against our own framework, one afternoon's work, was what finally surfaced it.
Start the token layer on day one
I built the visual system by repetition, which looked consistent and wasn't. The drift only became measurable once the colours had names, and by then it was 32 screens of cleanup.
Check the client's brand before choosing a palette
An obvious step I skipped. I spent a semester designing for a credit union in colours it has never used, and only caught it when I sat down to implement.
I drifted off my own brief before I caught it
My first pass at improvements was an acquisition story: join, share your plan, what membership is worth. Defensible screens, wrong problem. Re-reading our client deck showed the brief was confidence, not conversion. Three of those screens didn't survive the re-read, and cutting them was the right call.
Numbers are claims, not decoration
Six of my ten defects were a figure disagreeing with another figure. In a financial product that isn't a polish issue. It's the whole basis of whether anyone trusts the thing.

The lesson wasn't that I made mistakes — it's that a design file can look finished and still be wrong. Building it was the only thing that told me the difference, and I'd rather find ten defects in my own work than have a user find the first one.
